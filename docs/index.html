TL;DR

Reversing a tractor–trailer at speed is especially sensitive to modeling error: the passive trailer can rapidly jackknife, while friction, suspension, and terrain effects are only partially observed. We use a history-based neural dynamics model within MPPI to control the vehicle in BeamNG.tech, testing reverse speed targets of 30 and 50 km/h and a forward target of 80 km/h.

Across asphalt, snow, slope, and combined snow-plus-slope terrain, learned dynamics generally improve speed tracking and jackknife avoidance relative to a Fiala-tire analytic prior and three baseline controllers. Separate five-second rollout comparisons test whether giving the analytic prior access to local friction closes the prediction gap. Slope and banking are absent from the learned model’s training data.

Why tractor–trailers?

Reverse motion at speed makes uncertainty impossible to ignore.

Tractor–trailer systems offer a demanding setting for learned dynamics: the vehicle is nonlinear and underactuated, while the passive trailer can rapidly destabilize the overall system near its handling limits.

01

A passive trailer creates strong coupling

The tractor is actuated, but the trailer is not. Steering the tractor changes the hitch angle and trailer motion through a rigid mechanical coupling that is difficult to stabilize.

02

Reverse driving amplifies instability

In reverse, small errors in the predicted motion can compound quickly into hitch-angle growth. At higher speeds, there is less time to correct before jackknife or track departure.

03

Critical dynamics are hidden

Road friction, slope, banking, load transfer, and suspension behavior are difficult to measure online, yet they substantially change how the vehicle responds.

Most tractor–trailer research emphasizes parking, navigation, or collision avoidance; high-speed work usually assumes a fixed analytic model and rarely studies reverse motion. High-speed reverse driving over snowy, sloped terrain is therefore a direct test of whether learned dynamics can provide useful control predictions when the analytic prior is incomplete.

02 Contributions

Learning-based control at the handling limits.

01

02

03 Method

Learned rollouts for high-speed tractor–trailer control.

Our controller relies on learned state transitions that remain informative when high-fidelity vehicle effects and terrain variables are not directly measured.

Fully learned dynamics

An MLP with four hidden layers of 128 units consumes four steps of state-action history. It predicts linear and angular accelerations and realized control states; the dynamics are integrated with explicit Euler.

History instead of terrain measurements

Recent motion supplies context about unobserved operating conditions. The learned predictor receives no explicit friction, road slope, banking, or suspension state.

Train across driving conditions

Data collection varies friction and target speed, adds control noise and forward weaving, and iteratively adds trajectories collected by the learned controller near handling limits.

What the network learns

A direct prediction of vehicle response from recent motion. Training data are standardized, and the distinction between commanded and realized controls accounts for actuator response.

01

Observe

Each history step contains sine and cosine of the hitch angle, body-frame velocity, tractor and trailer yaw rates, and commanded and realized steering and acceleration.

02

Predict

The network outputs longitudinal and lateral acceleration, both yaw accelerations, and the next realized steering and acceleration states.

03

Initialize from motion

Four initial analytic-MPPI steps populate the history with observed motion, avoiding an artificial history that could fall outside the training distribution.

Analytic dynamics

A coupled tractor–trailer model with Fiala tire forces.

Alongside the learned predictor, we derive a reduced-order model with Fiala brush tire forces. It exposes the physical coupling at the hitch and serves as an interpretable analytic prior.

State and configuration

The model state includes tractor position and yaw, trailer yaw, longitudinal and lateral tractor velocity, and the two yaw rates. The hitch angle is the difference between tractor and trailer yaw.

Coupled equations of motion

A rigid hitch couples tractor and trailer acceleration: tire forces, inertial effects, and body-frame motion terms are solved together at every rollout step. This coupling is what makes reverse driving especially sensitive to modeling error.

Fiala-tire analytic prior

Lateral tire forces use a Fiala brush model, with slip-angle saturation governed by road friction, normal load, and cornering stiffness. This prior is used directly for baseline control and as the physics component of the residual formulation.

01

Rigid hitch constraint

The trailer velocity is induced by tractor motion and trailer yaw rate, so accelerations cannot be solved independently.

02

Coupled rollout update

At every rollout step, the coupled acceleration system is solved and then integrated forward to update the vehicle state.

03

Why learn the dynamics?

The reduced-order prior omits many soft-body, terrain, and powertrain effects present in BeamNG.tech. The fully learned predictor models the response directly from data.

Simulation settings

Four environments separate friction from terrain uncertainty.

The track follows the Circuit de Barcelona-Catalunya layout in BeamNG.tech. Snow and terrain modifications are added independently, using the same snow pattern and the same terrain wherever each appears. Both sloped settings test transfer to elevation changes and banking absent from training.

01

Asphalt

Uniform high-friction, flat pavement in the high-fidelity simulator. This nominal condition isolates unknown vehicle dynamics.

02

Snow

Noisy patches of low-friction snow interrupt an otherwise flat track, creating changing tire behavior under the same track geometry.

03

Slope

Uniform high-friction asphalt with elevation changes and banking. This isolates terrain variation from the effects of snow.

04

Snow + slope

The snow pattern and sloped terrain are combined, testing changing traction and unseen terrain together.

04 Results

More reliable reverse driving under uncertainty.

Learned-dynamics MPPI is compared with Fiala-prior MPPI, LQR-PID, IPOPT-based MPC, and MPCC. Each environment and velocity configuration has nine runs: three seeds at each of three starting locations. A completed run reaches 500 control steps, equivalent to 25 seconds.

All runs completed on flat tracks

The learned controller completes all nine runs at each of the three speed targets on both Asphalt and Snow. At the −50 km/h target, its mean speed is 47.2 km/h on Asphalt and 46.0 km/h on Snow, compared with 22.7 and 22.8 km/h for Fiala-prior MPPI.

Transfer to unseen slope and banking

On Slope, learned control completes 8/9 runs at each reverse target and 9/9 forward runs. It has lower RMS hitch angle than Fiala-prior MPPI at all three targets, although one −50 km/h run ends in jackknife.

Combined uncertainty remains difficult

On Snow + slope, learned control has no jackknife failures in the reported trials. Reverse stalls still limit completion to 6/9 runs at −30 km/h and 4/9 at −50 km/h; all nine forward runs complete.
